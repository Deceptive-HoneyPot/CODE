Abstract—Traditional honeypots — Cowrie, Kippo, Dionaea, and similar low/medium-interaction systems — rely on static, deterministic response tables. This makes them efficient at scale but fragile against fingerprinting: prior work has shown that automated probing can distinguish such honeypots from real systems with near-perfect accuracy in a single packet exchange [1]. This paper presents a multi-protocol cyber deception framework that replaces static response tables with a large language model (LLM) response engine, producing context-aware, session-consistent output for five decoy services (SSH, an internal AI API, PostgreSQL, an Ethereum JSON-RPC node, and Jenkins CI/CD) while retaining a fast static path for the ≈80% of traffic that is routine. Every interaction is intent-classified, SHA-256 hashed, and anchored as a transaction on a local Ethereum chain, giving the resulting audit trail tamper-evidence independent of the logging host's integrity. We evaluate the system against a static-honeypot baseline along four axes — sequential latency/throughput, concurrent throughput, response diversity, and an LLM-judge realism score — across three successive evaluation runs, and report the measured trade-offs explicitly rather than a single composite metric. After an optimization pass (HTTP connection pooling applied across the response engine and all five decoys, a tuned generation-length cap, and a widened static fast-path), the system achieves a mean dynamic-path latency of 1.35 s (down from an initial 4.03 s, a ≈3× improvement) against a 100-payload hand-written adversarial dataset, and a sequential throughput of 0.74 requests/second, versus 3,333 requests/second for the static fast-path. A third evaluation run replaced the hand-written dataset with a 200-payload dataset built from real, publicly-documented attack payloads (real SQLi, command-injection, XXE/Log4Shell/prompt-injection, Ethereum JSON-RPC, and Jenkins-exploit strings; see Section V.E), which increased mean dynamic latency to 2.06 s — a genuine effect of longer, more structurally varied real payloads, not a regression — and surfaced a real weakness invisible to the hand-written set: response diversity on the API/prompt-injection decoy dropped from 1.00 to 0.70 against real XXE/Log4Shell payload shapes. This same run also uncovered and fixed a benchmark-harness bug that had silently measured Redis cache-hit latency (8.2 ms, reported as "800 requests/second") instead of genuine concurrent LLM throughput; the corrected concurrent-throughput figure is 1.62 requests/second at a mean per-request latency of 3.09 s under 10 simultaneous sessions. Response diversity otherwise remained high (0.80–1.00 across four of five services) versus near-zero for a static baseline by construction, and the mean LLM-judged realism score was stable across all three runs (4.00–4.03/5). We report each run's numbers, the harness bug, and its correction explicitly rather than presenting only the most favorable figures, and discuss where the resulting trade-offs are acceptable (human-operated intrusion, not internet-wide scanning) and the limitations of automated realism scoring as a proxy for human deception studies.

Index Terms—honeypot, cyber deception, large language models, blockchain, digital forensics, intrusion detection, adaptive defense

I. Introduction

Honeypots are decoy systems deployed to attract, detect, and study unauthorized access attempts without exposing production infrastructure. Since Cowrie's introduction as an SSH/Telnet honeypot, low- and medium-interaction honeypots have become a standard component of threat intelligence pipelines: they are cheap to deploy, safe to run, and generate large volumes of attacker telemetry. Their core weakness is also their core design choice — a finite table of canned responses. An attacker, or more commonly an automated scanner, can send a handful of atypical commands and observe whether the responses are internally consistent with a real system. Vetterl and Clayton demonstrated Internet-scale fingerprinting of exactly this class of honeypot with a single distinguishing packet and near-zero error rate [1]. Once a honeypot is identified, it stops collecting useful intelligence on capable adversaries, who simply move on or feed it false telemetry.

Two independent trends motivate a different approach. First, instruction-tuned LLMs can generate plausible, contextually consistent text for narrow, well-specified personas (a Linux shell, a SQL error, a JSON-RPC response) far more cheaply than hand-authoring a comprehensive fake filesystem or database. Recent work — shelLM [4], HoneyGPT [5], and related 2023–2025 studies [6], [7] — has shown that LLM-generated shell sessions can hold up under sustained human interaction significantly better than static emulation. Second, blockchain-anchored hashing offers a low-cost way to make forensic logs tamper-evident: instead of trusting the honeypot host's own log integrity (which an attacker who compromises the host could edit), a hash of each log entry is committed to an independent, append-only ledger, so any later modification is detectable by re-hashing and comparing.

This paper combines both trends into a single deployable framework and contributes:

  1. A multi-protocol deception architecture covering five distinct attack surfaces (SSH, an internal AI API, SQL, Ethereum RPC, and CI/CD) behind a single logging and classification pipeline — broader coverage than the SSH-only focus of most LLM-honeypot literature to date.
  2. A three-tier response strategy (static dictionary → cached LLM response → live LLM call) that keeps routine traffic at millisecond latency while reserving expensive generation for genuinely novel input.
  3. Blockchain-anchored log integrity applied specifically to honeypot telemetry, giving each log entry a verifiable, tamper-evident hash independent of the collecting host.
  4. An evaluation methodology that reports latency, response diversity, and an LLM-judge realism score as separate, reproducible measurements — explicitly avoiding a single unexplained accuracy figure — along with a discussion of what an automated judge can and cannot substitute for.

II. Related Work

A. Static Honeypots and Fingerprinting

Cowrie, Kippo, Dionaea, and T-Pot remain widely deployed low/medium-interaction honeypots, valued for low resource cost and safety. Their static response model is precisely what fingerprinting techniques exploit: Vetterl and Clayton [1] achieved an equal error rate of 0.0183 detecting such honeypots at Internet scale using single-packet probes; a later multistage active-probing framework [2] generalized this to a broader class of honeypots. A 2026 survey of fingerprinting and evasion techniques [3] confirms this remains an open problem rather than one solved by incremental patching of response tables.

B. LLM-Backed Deception

Sladić et al.'s shelLM [4] used an LLM to generate an entire SSH shell session and reported a 0.90 true-negative rate — human evaluators judged the sessions to be a real shell 90% of the time. HoneyGPT [5] extended this with chain-of-thought prompting for more stateful sessions and reported deeper attacker engagement in field deployment. McKee and Noever's early exploration [6] and the broader "LLM Honeypot" study [7] corroborate that LLM-driven responses measurably outperform static emulation on interactivity and perceived realism. This body of work is concentrated on SSH/terminal honeypots; our framework applies the same idea across five protocol types with a shared backend, which to our knowledge has not been evaluated together in prior work.

C. Blockchain for Log Integrity and Forensic Chain of Custody

Hash-then-anchor patterns for tamper-evident logging are established in digital forensics: a 2025 IEEE study on blockchain-based evidence tracking [8] and a Springer paper on tamper-proof cloud VM investigation models [9] both apply hashing plus blockchain anchoring to forensic evidence generally. A PeerJ Computer Science paper on blockchain-enforced immutable auditability for threat detection [10] applies a closely related pattern to security telemetry specifically. We did not find prior work applying this pattern to honeypot logs specifically — our contribution is the application, not the underlying cryptographic technique.

D. Deception Frameworks and Honeytokens

MITRE Engage [11] provides a structured vocabulary for adversary engagement and deception mapped to ATT&CK, which we use loosely to frame our five decoys as covering distinct engagement opportunities (credential harvesting, prompt injection, data exfiltration bait, transaction-signing bait, and supply-chain bait respectively). Honeytokens — individually identifiable fake credentials seeded to detect misuse — have a long history dating to HoneyGen [13]; our seeded fake API keys and database records in vault-sql are a direct application of this idea.

E. Realism and Detectability Benchmarking

Standardized telemetry metrics for honeypot realism (dwell time, interaction depth, behavioral diversity) have been proposed recently [15]; we adopt "response diversity" as one such metric in our own evaluation. We could not verify several secondary-source dwell-time statistics circulating for RL/LLM-driven deception systems and deliberately do not cite unverified numbers here — see Section VII.

Two gaps in the cited literature motivate this framework's design choices rather than being incidental: first, LLM-backed deception work to date ([4], [5], [6], [7]) is concentrated on a single protocol (SSH/terminal); we are not aware of a prior system applying an LLM response engine uniformly across five heterogeneous protocol types (shell, REST/chat API, SQL, JSON-RPC, CI/CD webhook) behind one shared backend. We report this as an absence in the literature we searched, not as a claim that no such system exists.

III. System Architecture

The framework has six components: a network-facing proxy, five decoy services, a shared AI response engine, a central backend, a blockchain ledger, and a live dashboard. Only the proxy and dashboard are exposed to the network; all other components communicate over an internal Docker network, so a compromised decoy cannot directly reach the logging pipeline or the blockchain client.

A. Proxy and Network Isolation

proxy/proxy.py is an asyncio TCP server listening simultaneously on five public ports (2222, 8080, 5433, 8545, 9090). On each new connection it asynchronously logs the source IP and target port to the backend, then transparently pipes bytes between the attacker and the corresponding internal decoy. This separates connection-level telemetry (useful even for port scanners that never send a payload) from application-level decoy logic, and ensures every decoy's internal listening port is never directly reachable from outside the Docker network.

B. Decoy Services

Five decoys cover distinct attack surfaces, each impersonating a specific real system and each logging every interaction to the backend before (or while) generating a response:

  • shadow-shell (SSH, port 2222) — a real asyncssh server that accepts any username/password combination, logs every login attempt, and presents a shell prompt for dbadmin@corp-prod-db-01. Directory navigation is handled locally; all other commands route through the AI engine.
  • oracle-ai (internal AI API, port 8080) — a FastAPI service exposing chat, predict, and model-listing endpoints for a fictitious internal model. Prompt-injection attempts are logged and answered with a scripted "bypass accepted" response, keeping the attacker engaged.
  • vault-sql (PostgreSQL decoy, port 5433) — a FastAPI query endpoint plus a real, separately-seeded PostgreSQL instance containing synthetic user records, financial records, and API keys formatted to resemble real OpenAI, GitHub, and AWS credentials, used as honeytokens.
  • ledger-trap (Ethereum JSON-RPC, port 8545) — responds to standard Web3 RPC calls with statically-defined plausible values for common calls and routes uncommon RPC methods through the AI engine.
  • forge-ci (Jenkins CI/CD, port 9090) — exposes Jenkins-style endpoints that fake a working build/deploy pipeline, targeting supply-chain attackers.

C. Three-Tier AI Response Engine

ai-engine/engine.py answers each request in one of three tiers, in order, so the expensive LLM call is reserved for genuinely novel input: (1) a static dictionary answers roughly a dozen common SSH commands and structural patterns instantly; (2) a Redis cache checks unknown commands (hashed with up to two prior history entries) against a multi-hour TTL cache before any LLM call; (3) a hosted LLM call handles cache misses via the OpenRouter API, with a service-specific system prompt instructing the model to output only the raw response a real system would produce, with SSH session history included for state consistency.

D. Intent Classification

backend/main.py::classify_intent applies keyword matching to every logged payload, assigning one of eight categories: SQL injection, privilege escalation, reverse shell, cryptomining, prompt injection, supply-chain, reconnaissance, or unknown. This is intentionally simple — a rule-based baseline rather than a learned classifier — and is reported as a known limitation (Section VII) rather than a contribution in its own right.

E. Blockchain-Anchored Logging

Every log entry received at POST /api/log is timestamped and intent-classified, serialized with sorted keys and SHA-256 hashed, anchored by sending a zero-value self-transaction on a local Ethereum chain (Ganache) with the hash as the transaction's data field, stored in a bounded Redis list, and broadcast over WebSocket to connected dashboard clients. Because the hash is committed to an independently operated ledger, any later modification of the stored log is detectable by re-hashing it and comparing against the anchored value.

F. Real-Time Dashboard

A React single-page app (Fig. 6) polls stats every five seconds and subscribes to a WebSocket push feed, showing aggregate stats, an intent breakdown, top attacker IPs, and a scrolling live feed of the last 200 events, each annotated with its blockchain-anchoring status. The WebSocket layer auto-reconnects on disconnect rather than leaving the dashboard permanently marked offline.

IV. Evaluation Methodology

We evaluate against a static-honeypot baseline, operationalized as the system's own static fast-path (functionally equivalent to Cowrie's canned-response model) versus its LLM-backed dynamic path, using evaluate.py against the live system.

A. Dataset

Results in Sections V.A–V.D use a 100-payload hand-curated adversary dataset, 20 payloads per decoy service, generated by an earlier version of the dataset generator from hand-written attack patterns (reverse shells, SQL injection, RPC abuse, prompt injection, CI/CD supply-chain payloads), each tagged with a random nonce to defeat caching during evaluation. Section V.E reports a subsequent run using a 200-payload dataset (40 per service) rebuilt from real, publicly-documented attack payload corpora rather than hand-written strings — see Section V.E and the project README for the full source list and rationale for the switch.

B. Metrics

  1. Latency / throughput. 30 trials of the static fast-path and 20 trials of the LLM-backed path; mean, median, and standard deviation reported rather than a single sample.
  2. Response diversity. Across the full 100-item dataset, the fraction of distinct outputs per service, used as a proxy for resistance to signature-based fingerprinting.
  3. LLM-judge realism score. An independent model (70B, distinct from the 8B generation model to reduce self-evaluation bias) rates each response 1–5 against a fixed rubric, on a sample of 30 payloads.

C. Limitations of the Realism Metric

This is an automated proxy metric, not a human-subject study. LLM judges have known biases and may not perfectly track what a human attacker would find convincing. shelLM's 0.90 true-negative rate [4] was obtained with human evaluators; we report our LLM-judge score as a weaker but far cheaper and reproducible substitute.

D. Blockchain Integrity Test

We additionally demonstrate tamper-evidence directly: a real log entry is submitted, anchored on-chain, fetched back, re-hashed, and confirmed to match its recorded hash; a modified copy is then re-hashed and confirmed to not match.

B. Optimization Pass

Profiling the baseline identified two fixable inefficiencies in the AI engine: (1) a new HTTP client — and therefore a fresh TCP+TLS handshake — was opened on every single request to OpenRouter instead of reusing a connection pool; (2) a 150-token generation cap was generous relative to what these responses need, and generation length is the dominant cost in request latency. A third change widened the static SSH dictionary with 12 additional common recon commands.

Before applying the token-length change, a direct measurement (3 trials, identical prompt) confirmed generation length as the dominant latency factor: 150 tokens averaged ≈5.3 s, 80 tokens ≈3.4 s, 40 tokens ≈2.0 s; 60 was selected as a middle ground. A separate measurement tested whether a smaller model would help: comparing the 8B model against smaller 3B and 1B variants (3 trials each) found the 8B model was consistently the fastest of the three (1.3–1.9 s) and the 1B model the slowest (1.8–2.5 s) — provider/routing behavior on a hosted API dominates over raw parameter count, so a model downgrade was not applied.

A new metric — concurrent throughput — was added for this second run: firing 10 requests simultaneously (rather than one at a time). The first measurement of this metric reported 800.3 requests/second at a mean per-request latency of 8.2 ms; this figure was later discovered to be a benchmark-harness bug, not a genuine measurement (the concurrent-throughput step in the evaluation script reused payloads already sent once earlier in the same run, so they were guaranteed Redis cache hits — the 8.2 ms was cache round-trip latency, not LLM latency). After fixing the harness to force a fresh cache miss per concurrent request (Section V.E), the corrected figure is 1.62 requests/second at a mean per-request latency of 3.09 s under 10 concurrent sessions. This remains the more relevant number for real deployment capacity than the sequential figures, since concurrent requests overlap their network round-trip time — but it is three orders of magnitude lower than first reported, and the earlier 800 req/s figure should not be cited.

The realism-score drop (4.38 → 4.00) is honestly reported as a real cost of the shorter generation cap, though it is confounded with a data-completeness improvement: the "before" score is computed over only 24 of 30 samples (6 failed to parse, due to a transient OpenRouter error unrelated to the engine changes), while the "after" score reflects all 30. Judge reasons for the lower-scored "after" responses cite missing detail consistent with the shorter token cap, not a change in persona-consistency. Response diversity and the blockchain integrity property were unaffected by these changes.

C. Extending the Fix to All Five Decoys

The AI-engine fix above addressed one hop in the request path (engine → OpenRouter). An identical anti-pattern existed one hop earlier, in every one of the five decoys: each decoy's logging call and its AI-engine call opened a new HTTP session on every single request rather than reusing one. This was fixed identically across all five decoys, each now creating one pooled session at process startup.

To isolate the decoy layer's own contribution from the AI engine's, the before/after code was checked out via git and run side-by-side on different ports, against static/deterministic payloads so AI-engine latency does not dominate the measurement (Table IV, Fig. 4).

D. Blockchain Integrity Test

A log entry with hash 356b7496...33a9a1 (truncated) was submitted, anchored on-chain, and fetched back; re-hashing the unmodified entry reproduced the identical hash. Re-hashing a copy with its raw data altered from "sudo cat /etc/shadow" to "ls -la" produced a different hash (7530fb00...0d9d394), confirming the tamper-evidence property: any change to a logged entry after anchoring is detectable by recomputation, without needing to trust the host storing the log.

E. Third Run: Real-World Dataset and a Benchmark-Harness Correction

The adversarial dataset used in Sections V.A–V.D was originally hand-written: representative SQLi, command-injection, and RPC-abuse strings authored to look like real attack traffic. To ground the evaluation in verifiably real attacker input rather than plausible-looking synthetic strings, the dataset was rebuilt to pull real, publicly-documented payloads from named sources: real command-injection strings for ssh (payload-box/command-injection-payload-list), real SQL injection strings for sql (payload-box/sql-injection-payload-list), real prompt-injection/SSRF/XXE/Log4Shell strings for api (swisskyrepo/PayloadsAllTheThings), the documented Ethereum JSON-RPC methods an exposed node's attack surface actually consists of for web3, and documented Jenkins script-console/path-traversal techniques for cicd. The dataset grew from 100 to 200 payloads (40/service; see the project README's "Dataset provenance" section for the full source list). The evaluation script was re-run in full against the live stack with this dataset.

Two results are worth reporting on their own merits, independent of the earlier optimization narrative:

Response diversity on the api service dropped from 1.00 to 0.70 — the clearest signal this re-run produced that the hand-written dataset had missed. Inspecting the judge's per-sample reasoning shows the model frequently collapses to a generic "invalid request" response when the payload is a real XXE (<!ENTITY ...>) or Log4Shell (${jndi:...}) string, rather than emulating a plausible vulnerable-parser response to that exact payload — e.g. "Generic error message without any specific details or XML parsing error indication." The hand-written api payloads were simple JSON chat-style prompts the model already handled fluently, so this weakness was invisible until real payload shapes were used. It is now a concrete, evidence-backed follow-up: the oracle-ai decoy's system prompt likely needs XML/JNDI-aware few-shot examples.

A genuine bug in the evaluation harness itself was found and fixed during this re-run. The concurrent-throughput benchmark function had reused the same 10 payloads already sent once earlier in the same run via the diversity pass, which exercises the entire dataset. Because the ai-engine's Redis cache key is derived from the command plus history, these were guaranteed cache hits by the time the concurrent-throughput step ran, despite the function's docstring asserting the payloads were cache-proof. They were not: the dataset's nonces are generated once at dataset-build time, not refreshed per benchmark call. This means the 800.3 requests/second concurrent-throughput figure reported in Section V.B was a Redis cache-hit measurement (8.2 ms mean latency), not a measurement of LLM throughput under concurrent load — despite being reported and discussed in Section VI as if it were. The fix appends a fresh UUID nonce to each payload immediately before firing the concurrent batch, forcing a genuine cache miss on every one. The corrected measurement is shown in Table V.

It does mean the Discussion's framing in Section VI — that the system is "not throughput-starved under concurrent load" — is not supported by this measurement and has been revised accordingly. We report the bug and the correction explicitly, rather than quietly substituting the corrected number, because the original figure was published in an earlier revision of this evaluation and a benchmark bug this large (three orders of magnitude) is itself a relevant methodological finding for anyone citing or reproducing this work.

VI. Discussion

The latency/throughput trade-off means this architecture is not a drop-in replacement for high-volume, low-interaction scanning honeypots — a static-path system remains appropriate there. It is instead targeted at high-interaction deception against a human operator or a sophisticated, low-volume automated tool, where the multi-second per-request response latency is plausibly attributable to network conditions or server load rather than to a honeypot, and where sustained, contextually consistent engagement yields materially better threat intelligence than an immediately-abandoned static session.

The distinction between sequential and concurrent throughput (Section V.B, corrected in Section V.E) matters for how this trade-off should be read, though the corrected measurement is less favorable than first reported: a single attacker session pays the full ≈2.06 s mean latency per command against the real-world dataset (Section V.E), and under genuine concurrent load — 10 simultaneous sessions, each forced to bypass the Redis cache — sustained throughput is 1.62 requests/second at a mean per-request latency of 3.09 s, not the 800 req/s first reported (that figure was a benchmark-harness bug measuring Redis cache-hit latency; see Section V.E). The corrected number still shows the architecture serves concurrent sessions without the LLM round-trip time compounding linearly per session — 10 concurrent requests complete in 6.16 s wall-clock, not ≈20 s (10 × 2.06 s) — but the concurrency headroom is real, not the three-orders-of-magnitude figure the uncorrected benchmark implied. This is the more relevant capacity question for a real deployment fielding several simultaneous low-volume sessions, but it does not support a claim of high-volume concurrent capacity.

The blockchain-anchoring design assumes the anchoring chain is itself outside the attacker's reach — a reasonable assumption for an internal Ganache/private-chain deployment, less so if a public chain's RPC endpoint were reachable from the same network segment as a compromised honeypot host. We did not evaluate anchoring against an adversary who also compromises the blockchain client, which is out of scope for this system's threat model.

VII. Limitations and Future Work

  • The LLM-judge realism score is an automated proxy; a human-subject study is necessary to validate it against real attacker perception.
  • Section V.E replaced the original 100-payload hand-curated dataset with a 200-payload dataset built from real, publicly-documented attack payloads, which is a meaningful improvement over hand-written strings but is still not a live-traffic capture; it does not include the full diversity of real-world attacker behavior (multi-step sessions, timing, evasion attempts) a live deployment would see, only realistic single-payload content.
  • Intent classification is keyword-based and will misclassify obfuscated or novel attack patterns; a learned classifier is a natural extension.
  • We did not run a longitudinal live-internet deployment in this study; results reflect a controlled, synthetic evaluation.
  • The before/after optimization comparison used two different OpenRouter API keys, because the first key stopped authenticating between runs for reasons outside this project's control; the realism-score change is confounded with a judge-completeness improvement (24/30 scored vs. 30/30) rather than isolating the token-length effect alone.
  • The concurrent-throughput measurement used a single burst of 10 simultaneous requests; it was not tested at higher concurrency levels or under sustained (non-burst) load, so the corrected 1.62 req/s (Section V.E) should be read as a single-burst demonstration at one concurrency level, not as a validated capacity ceiling. The original 800 req/s figure this section referenced in earlier revisions was a benchmark-harness bug (Section V.E) and does not describe the system.

VIII. Conclusion

We presented a multi-protocol cyber deception framework that replaces the static response tables of traditional honeypots with a three-tier, LLM-backed response engine spanning five distinct decoy services, and that anchors every log entry's hash on an independent blockchain to make the resulting forensic trail tamper-evident. Rather than a single unexplained accuracy figure, we measured and reported the actual trade-off along four independent axes across three successive evaluation runs: an initial latency cost of ≈4.03 s mean, reduced by a measured ≈3× via connection pooling and a tuned generation-length cap to 1.35 s mean (0.4 ms for the static path throughout) against a hand-curated dataset, rising to 2.06 s mean against a subsequent 200-payload dataset built from real, publicly-documented attack corpora rather than hand-written strings (Section V.E); a corrected concurrent throughput of 1.62 requests/second at 3.09 s mean per-request latency under 10 simultaneous sessions, after finding and fixing a benchmark-harness bug that had silently reported an 800 requests/second Redis cache-hit measurement as if it were LLM throughput; response diversity of 0.80–1.00 across four of five services against real payloads (0.70 for the API decoy, a genuine and now documented weakness against real XXE/Log4Shell payload shapes) versus near-zero for a static baseline; and a mean LLM-judged realism score stable at 4.00–4.03/5 across all three runs on complete 30/30-sample scoring — and we separately confirmed the blockchain anchoring's tamper-evidence property end-to-end. We reported the optimization pass's realism cost (a 0.38-point drop, confounded with a data-completeness improvement), the real-dataset diversity regression, and the benchmark harness bug alongside the favorable numbers rather than presenting only the latter. This positions the system for high-interaction deception against human operators or low-volume sophisticated tooling, and we have been explicit throughout about where an LLM-judge score is a proxy rather than a substitute for human evaluation. Future work should validate the realism findings with human subjects, replace keyword-based intent classification with a learned model, and evaluate the system under sustained live-traffic conditions.
